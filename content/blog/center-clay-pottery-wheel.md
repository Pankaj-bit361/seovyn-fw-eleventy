A mound can look smooth while the wheel is stopped, then push against a fingertip once every rotation. That small pulse tells a beginner exactly what to work on before opening the clay.

To learn how to center clay on a pottery wheel, wedge and attach a mound without trapping air, then support the arms and apply steady pressure as the wheel turns. Cone the clay upward and compress it back down until its outline stops wobbling. A fingertip should feel steady through a full rotation before opening begins.

The sequence is repeatable, but the feel takes practice. A first wheel session can be productive even if most of it is spent learning to hold the clay steady rather than making a finished pot.

## Recognize centered clay and prepare to work

Centered clay turns with little or no side-to-side movement. Viewed from the side, the mound’s outline appears nearly still while the wheel spins. A lightly resting hand feels smooth motion instead of a rhythmic bump. [Claybook’s explanation of centered clay](https://claybook.studio/learn/what-centered-really-means/) uses both the visible outline and the feel of the clay as checks.

Start with preparation rather than trying to force an uneven lump into place. Wedge the clay to make its consistency more even, then shape it into a fairly symmetrical mound. In *Skilled Centering*, ceramic artist Joyce Michaud recommends fairly straight sides and a slight high point at the bottom. When that bottom point meets the wheel head or bat first, air has a route out as the rest of the base is attached.

Place the mound over the wheel’s center on a clean, dry surface and press it down firmly. It needs full contact with the wheel head or bat: air trapped underneath can make the clay release while throwing. Give the attached mound a gentle sideways nudge before switching on the wheel. If it shifts, stop and attach it again rather than trying to hold it in place with spinning hands.

Keep water and a sponge nearby. A little water lets the hands glide; a puddle or thick layer of slurry can make the clay too soft to control. Sit close enough that the arms do not have to reach forward, with feet supported and shoulders relaxed. Once centering begins, an elbow braced against the body or thigh—wherever it feels stable—helps the hands resist the clay’s repeated push.

## How to center clay on a pottery wheel: five steps

These five steps cover the movement from an attached mound to the point where it is ready to be checked. Work with steady hands; chasing each passing high spot usually makes pressure less consistent.

1. **Start the wheel and wet the contact points.** Set a speed at which the mound turns smoothly and the hands can stay planted. Dampen the clay and the parts of the hands touching it, without flooding the base.
2. **Establish the hand positions.** Put one supported hand against the side of the mound and the other across its top. The side hand guides clay inward; the top hand controls its height. Some demonstrations for a counterclockwise wheel place the left hand toward 12 o’clock—the point farthest from a potter seated at 6 o’clock. That is a reference position, not a requirement for every body or wheel direction.
3. **Apply firm, even pressure.** Press inward from the side while the top hand limits upward movement. Build pressure gradually, keep the wrists supported, and let each full rotation pass under the hands.
4. **Cone up.** Bring both hands toward the lower sides, squeeze inward, and allow the clay to rise between them. The hands travel upward with the moving clay rather than pulling it up.
5. **Compress down.** Return one hand to the side and use the other to press the cone downward into a rounded mound. The side hand keeps it from spreading too far outward. Release pressure gradually, then assess whether another cycle is needed.

The dominant hand may feel more precise on top, while another person may prefer it at the side. The useful arrangement is the one that lets both hands maintain contact without bouncing or straining. Once a comfortable position is found, keep it consistent for a few attempts so changes in pressure are easier to notice.

## What coning up and down changes

Coning is more than a way to reshape the mound. Moving clay upward from its lower sides and then compressing it downward helps bring the mass into a more even shape before the final steadying pressure. Michaud’s *Skilled Centering* emphasizes preserving the even consistency established by wedging; repeated working with excess water can weaken the clay rather than improve it.

Think of the upward movement as narrowing the lower mound, not pinching its tip into a sharp point. A very thin cone is harder to bring back down cleanly. During compression, the top hand sends clay downward while the side hand contains its outward movement. Keeping both hands connected to the clay through the change in direction makes the motion less abrupt.

Wheel speed should serve control. Some potters find a quicker rotation helpful while coning and reduce speed for the final compression; others need a slower start to keep their hands stable. If the clay pushes the hands out of position, reduce the speed or use a smaller mound. If the wheel turns so slowly that pressure arrives in separate jolts, increase it enough for a smooth, continuous pass. No single dial setting solves every centering problem.

## Check for wobble before opening

After a coning cycle, let the mound turn while keeping the hands nearby but free of heavy pressure. Watch one point on its outer edge for several rotations. It should trace the same outline rather than moving toward and away from the eye. This visual check separates the clay’s movement from an uneven mark on its surface.

Next, rest a fingertip lightly on top for **one full rotation**. Claybook recommends this tactile check: a pulse or bump means the mass is still off-center. The fingertip is a sensor here, not a tool for pushing the clay into place. If a pulse remains, return to supported side-and-top contact for another gentle compression, then release slowly and test again.

Opening is the next skill, not a centering fix. Pressing a hole into a mound that still shifts can leave one side thicker than the other, making later wall-pulling harder to control. A steady outline and feel are the cues to move on.

## Why the clay will not settle

When a mound repeatedly slips off-center, change one condition at a time. That makes each attempt more informative than simply pushing harder. The signs below help identify where to start.

| What happens | Likely issue | Useful adjustment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The mound lurches from the first turn | Its starting shape or attachment is uneven | Stop the wheel, reshape the mound, and reattach its base firmly |
| The hands bounce once per turn | The arms lack a stable brace | Move closer and support an elbow against the body or thigh |
| The clay catches against the fingers | The contact surface is too dry | Add a small amount of water where the hands touch |
| The mound becomes soft and hard to hold | Too much water or repeated working | Use less water and take a break rather than continuing to soak it |
| The clay pushes the hands out of position | Speed or mound size exceeds current control | Adjust the wheel speed or practice with a smaller mound |

Inconsistent pressure can cause trouble even when the setup is sound. If the hands press hard at each visible bump and relax between bumps, they may reinforce the wobble. Hold a supported position and build even pressure instead. Similarly, lifting the hands suddenly after compression can leave a new ridge; let the pressure taper off as the clay rotates.

A learner can understand every step and still need many attempts to feel the right amount of pressure. The useful measure of progress is whether the wobble becomes smaller or easier to diagnose, not whether every practice mound becomes a pot.

## Do you need a centering tool?

No specialty gadget is required for beginner pottery wheel centering. Wedged clay, a securely attached mound, water, and supported hands are enough to practice the technique. A sponge can help manage water, but it does not replace the side-and-top pressure that brings the clay into line.

A tool or change in setup may still help an individual potter. Someone who has difficulty maintaining a seated brace might benefit from a different seat height or an instructor’s help finding a comfortable hand position. A glove can change how the hand grips the clay, so it may call for an adjustment in water or pressure rather than the same approach used with bare hands. There is no need to buy a centering aid before finding out which part of the movement is difficult.

## What to practice after centering

Keep practice focused: prepare a mound, center it, perform the visual and fingertip checks, then try again. Short attempts leave room to notice whether attachment, arm support, or pressure made the difference. If the hands tire and begin to bounce, stopping is more useful than forcing another cycle.

Once the mound stays steady, opening creates the hollow at its center. Pulling walls comes after that and requires its own coordination; trouble with a wall does not automatically mean the earlier centering attempt failed. A beginner can work successfully through one step and still need practice on the next.

Hands-on feedback can shorten the guesswork because an instructor can see whether an elbow moves, a top hand lifts, or pressure changes mid-rotation. ## FAQ

### Which hand should control the side of the clay?

Use the hand that can maintain the steadier brace at the side; the other hand can control the top. For a counterclockwise wheel, a left-hand position near 12 o’clock is one reference used in instruction, but it is not mandatory. Try a position that lets the elbow stay supported and the wrist remain comfortable through a full rotation.

### What should happen if the clay slides on the wheel head?

Stop the wheel immediately instead of trying to catch the spinning mound. Remove it, clean and dry the attachment area, then reshape the bottom so it contacts the surface from the middle outward without trapping air. Press the mound down firmly before restarting. Clay that moves under a gentle sideways nudge is not ready to center.

### Can a two-hour class teach someone to center clay?

A two-hour class can introduce the hand positions, coning movement, and wobble check, with time to practice under an instructor’s eye. Consistent centering often takes further repetition. A useful first-session goal is to identify one adjustment—such as a steadier elbow or more even pressure—that makes the mound move less with each rotation.
